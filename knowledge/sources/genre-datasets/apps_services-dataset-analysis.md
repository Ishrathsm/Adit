# apps_services: what separates effective famous-brand ads (Pitt dataset, n=55)

Alright, team. Listen up. We've got a batch of 55 ads from our genre. The client wants to know what works, what doesn't, and how to write something that lands. I've broken down the viewer data. Pay attention, this is a masterclass in what real people take away from our work.

### 1. What the most effective ads (eff 5) have in common

The top-rated ads aren't just creative; they are surgically precise. They land one single-minded idea, and they use a creative device to make it unforgettable.

**Propositions are brutally simple:**
*   **Ease & Simplicity:** **Barclaycard's** "waterslide" isn't about finance; it's about life being as easy as paying with their card. Viewers said: "sliding it is as easy as life would be." **Mastercard's** "Elephant" ad has the same DNA: "so easy an animal could figure it out."
*   **A Specific, Powerful Function:** **webuyanycar.com** is genius in its bluntness. The proposition is the brand name. Viewers repeat: "They buy any car." **AdBlock** is equally direct: "it will block ads from my computer." No ambiguity.
*   **Consolidation:** **Google Play** didn't sell apps; it sold "entertainment in one place" that "will sync all of your devices."
*   **Empowerment:** **Google Chrome's** "Frank Restaurant" ad wasn't about browser speed; it was about how the internet can "help you run a small business successfully."

**Devices used to deliver the proposition:**
*   **Metaphor is king:** The most effective device here is the visual metaphor that dramatises the benefit. **Barclaycard's** waterslide for 'ease', **Google Analytics'** 'real-life site search' for 'understanding customer needs'. These are instantly understood.
*   **Humour that clarifies:** The humour in the best ads serves the point. **CareerBuilder's** "Monkeys" aren't just funny; they represent a terrible work environment you need to escape. The joke has a "because."
*   **Celebrity as a user, not just an endorser:** In **Mastercard's** ad, Peyton Manning isn't just a face; he's a fan, and the card facilitates his fandom. The viewer takeaway is that Mastercard "covers just about everything," not just "Peyton Manning uses it."
*   **Story & Emotion:** **Google Chrome** and **Haywards 5000** tell mini-stories of success and connection, powered by the service. This adds an emotional layer to a functional benefit.

**Tone:** Confident, clear, and often playful. They don't shout features; they demonstrate a core truth about the value they provide.

### 2. What the weakest (eff ≤ 3) do wrong

The weak ads fail in one of three ways:

1.  **Style over substance (The Squarespace Problem):** Look at **Squarespace's** ads ("Craig Ward," "Kevin Shum"). Viewers were "amazed" (exciting 0.8, 1.0) but completely confused. One said: "I don't know what this company is selling or does." They sold the aesthetic of creativity so hard they forgot to mention they sell websites. It’s a classic case of the creative work swallowing the message.
2.  **The joke/celebrity IS the ad:** In **American Express's** "Wes Anderson Ad," the only clear takeaway was "it is endorsed by Wes Anderson." The benefit—the "why"—is completely lost. Similarly, **E*Trade's** "Wasted $2 Million Bucks" ad was funny, but viewers gave vague reasons like "it will help with finances." The joke didn't land a specific proposition.
3.  **Clear but boring:** **Dragon's** "Anywhere Free Trial" ad was perfectly clear. Viewers understood it lets you "create and edit documents anywhere." But with 0% exciting and 0% funny, it had no creative hook to make it memorable or persuasive. It's a product spec sheet, not an ad.

### 3. The propositions viewers actually repeat back

When an ad works, viewers can articulate the "because" clause. These are the top 10 messages that cut through the noise in this dataset:

1.  **Because it makes [a task] easy and simple.** (Barclaycard, Mastercard)
2.  **Because it solves my specific annoyance [of X].** (AdBlock)
3.  **Because it brings all my [media/people] into one place.** (Google Play, Skype)
4.  **Because it will protect me/my money/my future.** (Allstate, Franklin Templeton)
5.  **Because it helps my business succeed/grow.** (Google Chrome)
6.  **Because it guarantees a specific outcome.** (webuyanycar.com)
7.  **Because it saves me money [on fees/purchases].** (E*Trade, Groupon)
8.  **Because it gives me access to things competitors don’t.** (Realtor.com)
9.  **Because it helps me find exactly what I want, quickly.** (Just Eat, Google Analytics)
10. **Because it associates me with success/a person I admire.** (SoftBank/Brad Pitt)

### 4. Do exciting / funny / particular sentiments help here?

Yes, but only as an amplifier, never as the main event.

*   The average "funny" score for eff 5 ads was **0.61**. The average "exciting" was **0.59**. These are high.
*   However, many weak ads (eff ≤ 3) also scored high on these metrics. **Berlitz's** "We Are Sinking" was 1.0 funny (eff 3). **Squarespace's** "Key and Peele" ad was 1.0 funny (eff 3).
*   **Conclusion:** Funny and exciting are jet fuel. Poured into a well-built engine (a clear proposition), they create massive thrust. Poured on the ground and lit (no clear proposition), you just get a fireball that signifies nothing.
*   **Sentiment:** "Amused" is the most common dominant sentiment for effective ads, linking directly to the use of humour. But notice the "inspired" and "confident" sentiments for **Google Chrome** and **Haywards 5000**. Tapping into empowerment and aspiration can be just as potent as a laugh. Fear, as seen in **Identity Guard's** "afraid" ad (eff 3), is a harder sell and may not be as effective as a positive, empowering solution.

### 5. Brand-specific patterns worth copying

*   **Mastercard vs. The Field:** Mastercard consistently finds simple, brilliant metaphors for ease ("Elephant") or belonging ("Priceless moments"). They don't sell card features; they sell what the card enables.
*   **Google's "Show, Don't Tell" Ethos:** **Google Play** shows devices syncing. **Google Chrome** tells a human story of its utility. **Google Analytics** uses a real-world metaphor. They make the abstract tangible, which builds both understanding and trust.
*   **The State Farm Platform:** "Like a Good Neighbor" is a repeatable comedic platform. The jingle is a trigger, the agent appearing is the gag, and the resolution is the benefit (service anytime, anywhere). It’s a masterclass in building a durable creative asset that reinforces the brand promise every single time.
*   **The Squarespace Cautionary Tale:** Avoid their pattern. They sell "cool," but their viewers don't know what they're buying. Their ads are for other art directors, not for potential customers. Contrast their abstract beauty with the grounded storytelling of Google Chrome's "Frank Restaurant." Both are stylish, but only one is effective.

### 6. 10 concrete rules for writing an ad in this genre

1.  **Start with the "Because."** Before you write a word, fill in this blank: "A viewer should use this service because ______." If you can't say it in one short, tangible clause, you don't have an idea.
    *   *Example:* For **AdBlock**, the "because" is "internet ads are annoying."

2.  **One Ad, One Idea.** Your ad must be about one thing. Ease. Security. Access. Pick one.
    *   *Example:* **webuyanycar.com** is about one thing only: "They buy any car." Not price, not service, not speed. One thing.

3.  **Dramatize the Benefit.** Don't just say "it's easy." Show me a man sliding through his office on a waterslide.
    *   *Example:* **Barclaycard's** "waterslide" is the perfect dramatization of "contactless payment is easy."

4.  **Make the Joke Serve the Point.** The punchline of the joke should be the product benefit.
    *   *Example:* **Mastercard's** "Elephant" ad. The punchline is that the card is so easy to use, an elephant can do it. The joke *is* the proposition.

5.  **Your VO/Copy Must Be Crystal Clear.** Even with a great visual, your copy needs to be direct. What are you offering?
    *   *Example:* **Google Play's** copy isn't poetic, it's descriptive: "Your music, movies, books and apps, available anywhere you go."

6.  **Show, Don't Just Tell.** Demonstrate the product in action or create a perfect metaphor for it.
    *   *Example:* **Google Analytics'** "Site Search" ad shows a clerk in a real store being unable to help, a perfect visual for a bad website search function.

7.  **Tone is Confident, Not Arrogant.** Your ad should feel like it knows it has the solution, without being pushy or condescending.
    *   *Example:* **Allstate's** "Back to Basics" has a calm, reassuring tone that says "we've been here, we'll be here, you can trust us."

8.  **The Celebrity is a Prop, Not the Star.** The celebrity should be used to illustrate the benefit, not be the benefit themselves.
    *   *Example:* **Mastercard** uses Peyton Manning as a fan whose passion is enabled by the card. This is much stronger than just "Peyton Manning uses Mastercard."

9.  **Your Tagline is the Viewer's Takeaway.** By the end, your tagline should sound like the viewer's own conclusion.
    *   *Example:* **State Farm's** "Like a good neighbor, State Farm is there" isn't just a jingle; it’s what viewers repeated back: "they are there for you."

10. **Test for Ambiguity.** Show your script to someone. If they can't immediately say what the service does and why it's useful, it’s not working.
    *   *Example:* **Squarespace's** "Craig Ward" ad fails this test spectacularly. A viewer said, "I don't know what this company is selling." Don't be that ad.

This is a small sample, so treat these as strong hypotheses, not gospel. But the patterns are clear. Clarity of proposition, amplified by a single-minded creative device, is what wins. Now, let's get to writing.
