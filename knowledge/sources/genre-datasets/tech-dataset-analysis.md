# tech: what separates effective famous-brand ads (Pitt dataset, n=76)

Alright, team. Let's break down what works in tech advertising. This isn't about shiny objects; it's about making people feel something and, more importantly, *do* something. Pay attention. This is how you win.

### 1. What the Most Effective Ads (eff 5) Have in Common

The top performers are ruthlessly single-minded. They don't throw ten features at the wall. They pick one, and they execute it brilliantly.

*   **A Crystal-Clear Proposition:** The message is so simple a viewer can repeat it verbatim.
    *   **Product Feature as Hero:** **ASUS's "#NoNeedToRush"** isn't about the phone; it's about its insane battery life, and viewers repeat back "because the batteries last much longer." **Samsung's "smart tv"** is about motion control. **Sky's "Quicksilver"** is about speed.
    *   **Solves a Specific Problem:** **Cingular's "bff Jill"** is about the pain of per-text fees, offering unlimited texting. **Havells "Father Daughter"** ad sells fireproof wires to address a primal fear for family safety.
    *   **Brand as an Identity:** When not selling a feature, the best sell a worldview. **Apple's "Crazy Ones"** isn't about a G3 Mac; it's about aligning the brand with genius and rebellion. Viewers say, "Because it thinks different."

*   **Effective Creative Devices:**
    *   **Demonstration:** The most powerful tool in this dataset. **GoPro's "Durability Commercial"** literally tries to break the camera. You don't have to *tell* us it's tough; you just showed us. **Samsung's "smart tv"** demo of motion control is the entire ad.
    *   **Metaphor:** When you can't demo a feeling, you create a metaphor for it. **Sony Bravia's** ads ("Ball Bounce," "Paint Ad") are the masters of this. They don't talk about pixel density; they flood a city with bouncing balls or exploding paint to *show* you "colour like no other."
    *   **Humour that Lands a Point:** The funny ads that work use humour to deliver the proposition, not distract from it. The **ASUS** ad's fast-rapping husband is funny *because* his wife's phone has a long battery. The joke serves the benefit. **Energizer's "Darth Vader"** ad uses the bunny to outlast a pop-culture icon of power, reinforcing "it keeps going and going."
    *   **Story & Emotion:** **Havells** uses a touching father-daughter story to make a wire feel like an act of love. Apple’s **iPhone 6 India TVC** isn’t about megapixels; it’s about capturing a moment with a loved one. The emotion provides the context for the technology.

*   **Tone:** The tone is always confident and purposeful, whether it's the "amused" confidence of **Microsoft** mocking its rivals or the "inspired" ambition of **Apple's "Crazy Ones."** There is no ambiguity.

### 2. What the Weakest Ads (eff ≤ 3) Do Wrong

The weaker ads commit one of two cardinal sins: they are either forgettably vague or memorably pointless.

*   **Vague, Generic Propositions:** They rely on tech clichés. **IBM's '86 commercial** gets "Because it is very innovative" from viewers. In tech, "innovative" is the price of entry, not a differentiator. The first **Apple commercial from 1977** gets "Because it's high tech." These are descriptions, not reasons to buy.
*   **"Borrowed Interest" That Goes Nowhere:** They use a celebrity, a funny gag, or a cool visual that completely eclipses the product.
    *   **HP's "Silly Students In A Nanjing University Elevator" (eff 1)** is the ultimate example. Viewers remember the funny video ("Because it promotes humor") but have no idea what HP is selling. The ad is entertaining wallpaper.
    *   **Best Buy's "Justin Bieber & Ozzy Osbourne" (eff 2)** is a mess. Viewers are confused: is it about 5G? A buyback program? The celebrities are the only thing people remember, not the offer.
    *   **Amazon's "BaldwinBowl Party"** gets "Because all the biggest celebrities are using it." This is a weak, indirect social proof, not a direct benefit to the viewer.
*   **Feature-Dumping without a "So What?":** They list features without translating them into a benefit. **Panasonic's LUMIX "4K Photo"** ad gets a weak "Because it helps you take clearer pics." Compare that to the laser-focused clarity of Cingular's "Because you can text all you want without having to pay extra."

### 3. The Propositions Viewers Actually Repeat Back

This is gold. This is what cuts through the noise. When you write your brief, you should be aiming for a "viewer's why" this clean.

1.  **Because it has a killer feature no one else has.** (e.g., Samsung TV's "motion control," Verizon Droid's "it can do a lot of things other phones can not")
2.  **Because its battery lasts forever.** (e.g., ASUS Zenphone's "battery that lasts 38 days")
3.  **Because it won't break.** (e.g., GoPro's "durable and waterproof," Caterpillar's "tough")
4.  **Because it works everywhere.** (e.g., Airtel's "good signal even in the mountains")
5.  **Because it's faster.** (e.g., Sky's "super fast" internet)
6.  **Because it has superior quality (colour, sound, etc.).** (e.g., Sony Bravia's "color like no other tv")
7.  **Because it saves me money / gives me more for my money.** (e.g., Cingular's "text all you want without having to pay extra")
8.  **Because it's safer.** (e.g., Havells' "wires will not catch fire")
9.  **Because it's better than the competition I know.** (e.g., Microsoft's and Samsung's anti-Apple ads)
10. **Because it's easy to use.** (e.g., Apple iPhone's "ITS EASY TO USE," Xerox's "so easy... anyone can use it")

### 4. Do Exciting / Funny / Particular Sentiments Help Here?

Yes, but they are amplifiers, not a substitute for a proposition.

*   **Excitement:** The average `exciting` score for eff 5 ads is **0.67**, while for eff ≤ 3 ads it's **0.58**. A slight edge, but look closer. Many weak ads are rated as highly exciting (e.g., **LG's "David Warner" at 1.0**). Excitement gets you watched, but it doesn't guarantee persuasion. An exciting demo of a real benefit (**Sky "Quicksilver," eff 5, exciting 1.0**) is effective. An exciting but pointless visual (**LG "David Warner," eff 3, exciting 1.0**) is not.
*   **Funny:** The average `funny` score for eff 5 ads is **0.63**, while for eff ≤ 3 ads it's **0.61**. Again, almost identical. The difference is *how* humour is used. **GoPro's "Owl Dance-Off" (eff 3, funny 1.0)** is hilarious but vague. **ASUS's "#NoNeedToRush" (eff 5, funny 1.0)** uses humour to land its battery life message.
*   **Sentiment:** "Amused" shows up constantly in effective *and* ineffective funny ads. "Alert" and "Active" appear often with effective ads demonstrating new features or capabilities (**Centel**, **GoPro**). The sentiment is the *result* of a good strategy, not the cause of it.

**Conclusion:** Don't brief for "funny" or "exciting." Brief for a clear proposition. Then, use humour or excitement as a tool to make that proposition more memorable and persuasive.

### 5. Brand-Specific Patterns Worth Copying

*   **Apple vs. Samsung (The Boxer vs. The Brawler):**
    *   **Apple** sells the user's identity and the emotional outcome. **"Crazy Ones"** is about the user being a creative genius. The **iPhone 6 TVC** is about love and memory. They sell the *feeling* of owning Apple.
    *   **Samsung** sells the product's superiority, often by attacking Apple directly. **"Galaxy S3"** mocks iPhone users for not having features like a big screen or easy sharing. They sell the *features* that make them a smarter choice. Both are eff 5 strategies; they just appeal to different mindsets.

*   **Sony Bravia (The Master of Metaphor):** Sony owns "colour." Instead of talking specs, they create breathtaking visual metaphors (**"Ball Bounce," "Paint Ad"**). This is how you take a rational benefit (better colour gamut) and make it emotionally resonant and unforgettable.

*   **GoPro (Selling the Verb, Not the Noun):** The best GoPro ads (**"Baby Skateboarder," "Travis Rice"**) don't sell a camera; they sell the ability to capture amazing moments. They focus on the *result* of the product. Their effectiveness drops when they forget this and just try to be cute (**"Owl Dance-Off"**).

### 6. 10 Concrete Rules for Writing a Tech Ad

1.  **Start with the "Because..."** Before you write a line of copy, finish this sentence: "The viewer should choose us because..." If the answer isn't a single, simple benefit, your idea is too complicated. **Example:** Cingular's "bff Jill" is built entirely on "Because you can text all you want without paying extra."
2.  **Demonstrate. Always.** Tech is abstract. Make it tangible. Show, don't tell. **Example:** The entire **GoPro "Durability Commercial"** is a demo. No VO needed.
3.  **If You Can't Demo, Metaphorize.** Turn your abstract benefit (speed, power, colour) into a stunning visual. **Example:** **Sony Bravia's "Ball Bounce"** is a metaphor for vibrant colour.
4.  **Make Humour Do a Job.** The joke must reveal the proposition. If you can lift the joke out and the ad still makes sense, the joke is a parasite. **Example:** In the **ASUS** ad, the wife's long battery life is the punchline to the husband's frantic rapping.
5.  **Find Your Enemy.** Your enemy can be a competitor, a problem, or an old way of doing things. It focuses your message. **Example:** **Verizon's "iDon't"** ad defined the Droid by everything the iPhone at the time couldn't do.
6.  **Translate Features into Human Benefits.** No one buys a "tri-band gigabit router." They buy an end to family fights over Wi-Fi. **Example:** **Havells** doesn't sell "flame retardant coating"; it sells keeping your daughter safe.
7.  **Choose Your Battlefield: Brand or Product.** Are you selling a specific feature, or are you selling an identity? Don't mix the two in one 30-second spot. **Example:** **Apple's "Crazy Ones"** sells an idea. **Samsung's "smart tv"** ad sells a feature. Both work.
8.  **Your VO/Copy Should Clarify, Not Confuse.** The copy should support the visual, not fight it. Often, less is more. **Example:** The only copy in the **Energizer "Darth Vader"** ad that matters is "It keeps going and going..."
9.  **Write the Tagline First.** A great tagline is your proposition distilled into its most potent form. It should be the final thought you leave with the viewer. **Example:** "Colour like no other." (Sony Bravia)
10. **Embrace a Confident Tone.** Whether you're funny, emotional, or aggressive, be decisive. Weak ads feel hesitant. Strong ads know exactly why they deserve to exist. **Example:** The swagger of **Microsoft's "Don't Fight"** ad leaves no doubt about its message: we're the smart choice above the fray.

Now, go write something that makes a difference. And remember the sample-size caveat; this is directional, not gospel. But the direction is clear: clarity is king.
