# Ad Copywriting Principles (all genres)

This is how to write the idea, the script, the voice-over, the supers and the tagline for a video ad. It's drawn from already-analysed research, not from our own guesses:
- Google/Ipsos ABCD (5,000+ ads);
- System1 and Orlando Wood's *Lemon*;
- Binet and Field / IPA;
- Ehrenberg-Bass (Romaniuk);
- Ogilvy's TV rules;
- the Pitt ad dataset, filtered to famous-brand ads;
- famous-brand case studies.

Genre specifics are in `genres/`.

## 1. The idea comes before the words

- **One single-minded proposition.** Every top-rated famous-brand car ad in the Pitt data left viewers with one reason they could repeat in their own words: "because it's reliable", "because it's like a bullet", "because it gives you confidence". The lowest-rated ones had vague claims such as "trendsetter" or "the world sedan", and viewers couldn't say why they should buy. **Test it:** can a stranger finish "I should buy X because…" after one viewing?
- **Show the proposition; don't say it.** Use one of these devices:
  - a **demonstration** (Volvo "Epic Split" showed its steering precision; a VW Beetle floats);
  - a **metaphor** (BMW M5 "Bullet"; Honda "Cog", where the parts just work);
  - a **story with a small truth** (Google "Parisian Love", told in searches);
  - **humour or absurdity** (Old Spice, Audi "Green Police", Snickers);
  - **radical honesty** (VW "Think Small", "Lemon").
- **Emotion beats rational persuasion over the long term** (Binet and Field). Still, a simple, tangible functional message can work across the funnel (ABCD), and the best ads **deliver the functional message through an appealing story**.
- **Start from a truth** about the buyer or the product (System1 "showmanship"). Examples: Dove found that 4% of women called themselves beautiful; Old Spice found that 60% of body wash is bought by women; Snickers used "you're not you when you're hungry".

## 2. Structure

- **Hook the first 5 seconds** with something surprising, unexpected or delightful (ABCD).
  - Use **tight framing** on the product or people, and **more than two frames in the first 5 seconds**.
  - If people feature, open with them on screen. Ogilvy puts it as "open with the fire".
- **Show the brand early** (ABCD: in the first 5 seconds; Ogilvy: within 10), because many viewers remember the ad but not the brand, or credit it to a competitor. Integrate the logo into the story or onto the product, or run it as a **persistent watermark** (all three reference bike ads did).
  - Exception: a **big reveal** held to the end can work when the whole ad is a riddle about the brand (Honda "Cog", Cadbury "Gorilla"). That only works for a very famous brand whose assets are already known.
- **Let a scene unfold** rather than a montage. Wood and System1 found that "right-brain" features win:
  - characters;
  - dialogue;
  - a scene playing out;
  - a sense of place;
  - humour;
  - cultural references.

  "Left-brain" features are rising but weaker: rhythmic rapid cuts, obtrusive text, disconnected scenes, a voice-over over a montage. Ogilvy likewise found that too many scene changes lower brand preference. **So: fewer, cleaner shots that build one scene, not a montage.**
- **The classic arcs:** hook → problem → solution → CTA → tagline; or intrigue → reveal → payoff. Pitt and research datasets also use Freytag's arc (exposition, rising action, climax, resolution) and mark the **climax** of each ad.

## 3. Voice-over and dialogue

- **Word budget:** about 2.5 words a second at a conversational pace, so about 38 words in 15s, 75 in 30s and 150 in 60s. A slow luxury read runs at about 2 words a second (20% fewer words); a brisk youth read can top 3. Numbers count as several words.
  - For ads where the picture leads, write less: 20–40 words in 30s, or none at all.
- **Short lines, one idea per line,** with room to breathe. Write for the ear: contractions, and no stacked clauses.
- **On screen beats voice-over:**
  - People speaking on camera hold attention better than a voice-over (Ogilvy).
  - A brand mentioned by someone on screen lands better than one in a plain voice-over (ABCD).
  - In our faceless genres, the picture should carry the story and the voice-over should be sparse.
- **Supers:** reinforce the strongest claim with text on screen, using **exactly the same words as the voice-over** (Ogilvy). Keep text from obtruding (Wood).

## 4. Brand assets and "fluent devices"

- **Distinctive assets:** a colour, sound, character, shape, font or tagline that **people link to only you** (uniqueness) and that **many people know** (fame). Build them by repeating them without changing them (Romaniuk). Examples: Audi "Vorsprung durch Technik" (market share ×5 over the campaign), Intel's sound, the Coca-Cola script.
- **A fluent device** is a recurring character or scenario across executions, such as the Geico Gecko or Kia's hamsters. Campaigns with one are significantly more likely to grow share and profit, and they reduce price sensitivity (System1). **For every new brand we make, propose one** (a character, a sound, a recurring shot or a line) and reuse it.
- **A sonic logo or mnemonic**, used in every ad. Ogilvy: "employ memory aids".

## 5. Call to action and end card

- **Be specific and single:** "Book a test ride", "Order now", "Download free" beat "Learn more". Make it **heard and seen** (ABCD). Add urgency with the word "limited" when there's a real offer.
- **End on a clean pack or hero shot** with the product prominent (Ogilvy: showing the package shifts preference more).

## 6. Taglines

- **Under about 8 words;** the product's promise or its attitude.
- **The best taglines become shared language:** Audi's "a bit more Vorsprung", Honda's "Isn't it nice when things just… work?", Snickers' "You're not you when you're hungry", Harley's "Live to Ride".
- **Use heritage or truth when you have it** (Vorsprung came from an old Audi factory brochure).
- **Avoid:** generic lifestyle lines ("Where innovation meets freedom"), claims every rival could make ("the perfect SUV"), and dated sounds.

## 7. Checklist before any script goes to the user

1. Can a stranger say "I should buy X because…" in one sentence after one viewing?
2. Is the proposition **shown** through a demonstration, metaphor, story or humour?
3. Does the brand appear in the first 5 seconds, or is the whole ad deliberately a riddle about a famous brand?
4. Is there a hook in the first 5 seconds, with tight framing and more than two frames?
5. Does a scene unfold, rather than a montage of disconnected shots?
6. Is the voice-over within the word budget, with supers matching it word for word?
7. Is there a distinctive asset or fluent device we can reuse?
8. Is the CTA specific, heard and seen?
9. Is the tagline 8 words or fewer, specific to us, and something that could become shared language?
